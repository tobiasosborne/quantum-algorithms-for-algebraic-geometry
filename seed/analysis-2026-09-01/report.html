Projective varieties as ground spaces: what the construction is, what it can compute, and realistic conjectures

Analysis of the handwritten notes page81.tex (complex projective varieties and quantum mechanics). Sections 1–3 fix the setting and identify the construction; 4–7 assess the quantum-algorithmic content; 8 collects the conjectures, separated from what is already a theorem; 9 lists corrections to the notes. Everything marked numerically was checked by exact diagonalisation (scripts in the session scratchpad, numerics/); everything in Section 8 survived one adversarial referee pass, which killed or weakened about a third of the first draft's claims.

1. Setting, made precise

Let R=C[z0,…,zn]=⨁NRN with RN the homogeneous polynomials of degree N, dimRN=(N+nn). Three inner products appear in this business.

name‖zk‖2adjoint of zj
Fock / Fischer (Gaussian measure on Cn+1)k!:=∏jkj!∂j exactly
Bombieri–Weyl / Kostlan / Drury–Arvesonk!/N!∂j up to a degree-dependent scalar
sphere S2n+1 (the notes' normalisation)n!k!/(N+n)!∂j(D+n)−1, D = degree operator

Within a fixed RN these differ by a scalar, so orthogonal complements, kernels of homogeneous differential operators and the ground spaces below coincide in all three. They are not the same Hilbert module across degrees: norms of the graded direct sums, boundedness of the shifts, and everything in the Drury–Arveson dictionary (Section 2) depend on which one is used. I use the Fock form throughout: |k⟩=zk/k! is orthonormal, aj=∂j, aj†=zj, [ai,aj†]=δij. Everything is then literally n+1 bosonic modes, and RN is the N-boson sector,

RN≅SymN(Cn+1)=H0(Pn,O(N)),

the symmetric subspace of N qudits of dimension n+1 (not n), i.e. the ambient space of the N-th Veronese embedding. The notes' L2(CPn) should be read as the Hardy space of the sphere or as symmetric Fock space; the genuine L2(CPn) is a different, non-holomorphic space.

For a homogeneous f=∑αfαzα of degree m put

a†(f)=f(a†)=multiplication by f,a(f):=a†(f)†=f¯(∂)=∑αfα‾∂α.

The conjugation on the coefficients is forced by self-adjointness; the notes write f(∂), which agrees only for real f.

2. The ground space is Macaulay's inverse system (this part of the notes is correct and classical)

For I=(f1,…,fd) homogeneous with degfj=mj, IN=∑jfjRN−mj, define

H=∑j=1da†(fj)a(fj),HN:=H|RN.

Fact 2.1 (frustration-free; kernel = orthogonal complement of the ideal). Since kerM†=(ranM)⊥,

kerHN=⋂jkera(fj)|RN=⋂j(fjRN−mj)⊥=(IN)⊥,dimkerHN=(N+nn)−dimIN=HFR/I(N).

The ground-state degeneracy of HN is the Hilbert function of R/I; for N beyond the regularity it is the Hilbert polynomial, whose degree is dimV(I) and whose leading coefficient is degV(I)/(dimV)!. The range of HN is IN itself, so the projector onto IN is the step function Θ(HN). Numerically: dimkerHN agreed with an independent exact rank of the Macaulay matrix for the conic (2N+1), the twisted cubic (3N+1), the rational normal quartic (4N+1), a monomial ideal, two generic quadrics in P3 (4N) and the boolean ideals of Section 6, and span{zβfj}⊥kerHN held to 10−14.

Fact 2.2 (identification). (IN)⊥={q∈RN:f¯(∂)q=0∀f∈I} is the degree-N part of the Macaulay inverse system of I¯ (apolarity; Macaulay 1916, Iarrobino–Kanev 1999), and for a principal ideal RN=fRN−m⊕kerf¯(∂) is Fischer's decomposition (Fischer 1918; Shapiro 1989). For general I the orthogonal decomposition RN=IN⊕IN⊥ is elementary, but the identification of the complement with the inverse system is the useful content. The map (R/I)N→(IN)⊥, [q]↦P0q, is an isomorphism, so the ground space is the graded quotient ring as a vector space; its multiplicative structure is the compression of the creation operators, P0,N+1aj†P0,N= multiplication by zj on (R/I)N→(R/I)N+1.

Operator-theoretic dictionary. With the Bombieri–Weyl norm the graded sum ⨁NRN is the Drury–Arveson space Hn+12, Arveson's d-shift is Sj=aj†/Nˆ+1 (the creation operator normalised to a contraction), the closure of I is a graded submodule, and ⨁N(IN)⊥ is the quotient module Hn+12/[I] on which the compressed Sj act. Arveson's curvature invariant equals the Euler characteristic for graded modules (Arveson 2000) and, by Fang (2003), is the leading multiplicity of the Hilbert polynomial of R/I; the dimension of the module is degpR/I+1. The Arveson–Douglas conjecture (essential normality of these compressed shifts, Schatten class p>dim of the affine cone) is proved for principal, monomial, one-dimensional and radical-linear-union cases and for varieties smooth away from the origin (Engliš–Eschmeier 2015; Douglas–Tang–Yu 2016), and open in general as of 2025. None of this is new, but it is exactly the mathematics of the ground spaces here, and the notes' objects have standard names there.

Fact 2.3 (the notes' worked example). For f=z0z1−z22: a(f)z0z1z22=z22−2z0z1 and a(f)(z0z1+cz22)=1−2c, so c=1/2, both as in the notes. The line "1−2=?−1" has a sign slip: a(f)f=∂0∂1(z0z1)+∂22(z22)=1+2=3=‖f‖2. In degree 2, dimkerH2=5=2⋅2+1.

3. What kind of "local" Hamiltonian this is

Under RN≅SymN(Cn+1) (unitarily, |k⟩↦ the normalised symmetrisation of |0⟩⊗k0⋯|n⟩⊗kn), a degree-m form f corresponds to the symmetric tensor

|F⟩=∑αfαα!m!∑w:content(w)=α|w⟩∈Symm,⟨F|F⟩=∑α|fα|2α!m!=‖f‖BW2,

so the Bombieri–Weyl norm is the natural tensor norm. On symmetric states aj1⋯ajm=N!/(N−m)!⟨j1⋯jm|1..m⊗1, hence

HN≅Psym[∑jmj!∑|S|=mj(|Fj⟩⟨Fj|)S]Psym,

a permutation-symmetric sum of rank-one mj-body positive terms (‖fj‖BW2 times a projector), compressed to the symmetric subspace; individual subset terms do not preserve SymN, only the symmetrised sum does. This is a bosonic analogue of quantum k-SAT: forbidden mj-body states |Fj⟩, but restricted to the symmetric sector and with every forbidden state replicated over all subsets. It is not a Bravyi instance on distinguishable particles. In physical terms the generators are number-conserving multi-boson interactions: for the conic, H=n0n1+n2(n2−1)−(a0†a1†a22+h.c.), which is the spin-mixing Hamiltonian of a spin-1 spinor condensate in the single-mode approximation (Law–Pu–Bigelow 1998, with (z0,z1,z2)↔(a+1,a−1,a0)); its ferromagnetic phase has exactly 2N+1 degenerate ground states, the Hilbert function of the conic, so this quadric ideal has already been realised in the laboratory. "Local" here means: each term moves mj bosons (it can change up to 2mj occupation numbers) and is mj-body on the qudit register. It is not local on the qubit register a quantum computer would use (occupation numbers in binary); there HN is a sparse, efficiently row-computable operator, which is the right notion for QSVT (Section 5).

Consequences.

  • Points of the variety are the product states in the ground space. The normalised spin-coherent state |p⟩⊗N, ‖p‖=1, is the polynomial (p⋅z)N/N!, and the reproducing property ⟨(p⋅z)N,q⟩=N!q(p¯) gives
|p⟩⊗N∈kerHN⟺q(p¯)=0∀q∈IN⟺p¯∈V(IN),

with V(IN)=V(I) for N≥maxjmj. A point x∈V(I) corresponds to the physical state |x¯⟩⊗N. The energy of a coherent state is a weighted sum of squares of the equations,

⟨p⊗N|HN|p⊗N⟩=∑jN!(N−mj)!|fj(p¯)|2,‖p‖=1,

numerically exact to 10−15, conjugation included (a complex quadric and a point with f(p)=0≠f(p¯): the state of p has energy O(N2), the state of p¯ has energy 10−16).

  • Reduced structure. span{|p⟩⊗N:p¯∈V}=(I(V)N)⊥ with I(V) the vanishing ideal. So the ground space is spanned by coherent states of points of V exactly when IN=I(V)N (degreewise; global radicality and saturation need this for all large N). Otherwise the ground space contains dimI(V)N−dimIN additional, necessarily entangled, states carrying non-reduced or irrelevant-torsion structure.

  • Multigraded (Segre) version. With variables zi,s, i=1..n (sites), s=0..q−1 (levels), the multidegree (1,…,1) sector of C[zi,s] is (Cq)⊗n with z1,s1⋯zn,sn↔|s1⋯sn⟩, orthonormal (all Fock norms are 1). A multilinear form f on a set S of sites gives

a†(f)a(f)|(1,…,1)=|f⟩⟨f|S⊗1

exactly, no factorials (checked to machine precision for 3 to 12 sites; e.g. three generic bilinear forms on the three pairs of a 3-qubit system leave a 2-dimensional inverse system, three singlet projectors leave the 4-dimensional spin-3/2 multiplet). Hence every quantum k-SAT instance is the (1,…,1) block of an ideal generated by k-multilinear forms: a rank-r local projector is split into r rank-one ones, which preserves the Hamiltonian and its gap. Product-state solutions are the points of the multiprojective variety V(I)⊂(Pq−1)n; entangled solutions are the rest of the inverse system in that multidegree.

Proposition 3.1 (translation of the QSAT theorems). For ideals generated by k-multilinear forms across n blocks of q variables, the promise problem "HFR/I(1,…,1)>0, or the smallest eigenvalue of H(1,…,1) is ≥1/poly(n)" is in P for k=2, q=2 (Bravyi 2006; for bilinear forms across blocks of unequal sizes such as (2,5) it is already QMA1-complete, Rudolph–Gharibian–Nagaj 2024) and QMA1-complete for k=3, q=2 (Gosset–Nagaj 2013); with monomial generators it is classical k-SAT, NP-complete for k≥3. Without the NO-gap promise, exact vanishing of the Hilbert function at (1,…,1) is not thereby placed in QMA1. The geometric question V(I)≠∅ (product-state satisfiability) is, by the Nullstellensatz, non-vanishing of the Hilbert function in all large multidegrees, not just at (1,…,1). This is the cleanest statement of what "encoding a variety in a ground state" buys: the Hilbert function at the multilinear degree is the linear-algebra relaxation of the geometric question, and it is already quantum-hard.

4. The notes' second half: a parent Hamiltonian for the ideal itself

The notes try to build H′ with kerH′=IN (rather than IN⊥) by summing shifted projectors, H↦∑αJαHJα†, and correctly notice an AND/OR problem. The obstruction is exact: the kernel of a sum of positive operators is the intersection of the kernels (AND), whereas IN=spanβ{zβfj} is a span (OR); ⋂αJα(kerH0) is far too small.

Two notions of locality must be kept apart, and the answer is negative in both, except when the whole ideal lives in boundedly many modes.

Fact 4.1 (no k-body parent Hamiltonian for IN). Let 0≠IN≠RN and N≥k+minjmj. If T≥0 is a k-body operator on the symmetric sector with T(IN)=0, then T=0 on SymN. Proof sketch: T vanishes on the joint support of the k-body marginals of IN; a vector u∈Symk orthogonal to that support satisfies, by the contraction formula of Section 3, u¯(∂)(fh)=0 for a generator f and all h∈RN−m. Take h=ℓN−m with ℓ a linear form, u(ℓ)≠0, ℓ∤f (possible since u≠0 and f has finitely many linear factors); in coordinates with ℓ=z0 the coefficient of the lowest power z0N−m−k in u¯(∂)(fz0N−m) is (N−m)ku(ℓ)‾f|z0=0≠0. So the marginals have full support and T=0. This applies to monomial ideals too: for I=(z0) the projector PIN⊥=(1−|0⟩⟨0|)⊗N is N-body, and θ(nˆ0) is not fixed-body local.

Fact 4.2 (few-mode parents exist only for ideals confined to few modes). In the mode (second-quantised) sense, if I is generated by forms in the modes of a bounded set A (after a unitary change of variables), then IN=⨁r(I∩R(A))r⊗R(Ac)N−r and 1−PI∩R(A) is an |A|-mode operator (an unbounded function of the mode operators, legitimate as an on-site term in a Bose–Hubbard sense, but not a local Hamiltonian in the complexity-theoretic sense) with kernel IN. Conversely, a common kernel of positive operators each supported on a bounded mode set is an intersection of few-mode conditions, while IN=∑j(fj)N is a union-type (OR) condition: for a monomial ideal whose generator supports cannot be covered by any of the allowed mode sets, e.g. (z0z1,z2z3,…,z2rz2r+1) with r large, any few-mode T≥0 annihilating IN also annihilates every |k⟩ with at least two bosons outside its mode set (extend k outside the set to occupy a generator), so the common kernel is strictly larger than IN. Note the property is not invariant under change of generators, e.g. (z0+z1,z0−z1)=(z0,z1) is confined to two modes.

What is available:

  • PIN=Θ(HN) by QSVT, at cost O(α/ΔN⋅logϵ−1) uses of an α-normalised block encoding of HN (Section 5). Non-local, but efficient whenever ΔN/α is not too small.
  • The "unitary version of Lf†" the notes ask for is the isometry in the polar decomposition Mf=Uf|Mf| of multiplication by f, Mf:RN−m→RN; Uf maps RN−m isometrically onto (f)N and is a QSVT singular-value transformation of Mf, at cost σmax/σmin of Mf, which by Fact 7.1 is O(Nm/2).
  • The shift operators Jj of the notes are the isometric parts of aj†=Jjnˆj+1, unilateral shifts on ℓ2(Z+). A graded subspace invariant under all aj† is exactly a homogeneous ideal, since a graded subspace closed under multiplication by each zj is an ideal; invariance under the Jj is a different condition (the occupation-dependent factor nˆj+1 does not preserve a general ideal). Without the grading the invariant-subspace lattice is larger (Beurling's θH2 in one variable), and this is Arveson's Hilbert-module setting. Passing to ℓ2(Z) (Laurent polynomials) makes the shifts unitary but destroys positivity of the grading and the Fock structure.

5. What a quantum computer can do with HN: quantum linear algebra on Macaulay matrices

Let ΦN:⨁jRN−mj→RN, (hj)↦∑jfjhj be the degree-N Macaulay map in Fock norms. Then HN=ΦNΦN†, kerΦN=Syz(I)N, and

ΔN:=λmin≠0(HN)=σmin≠0(ΦN)2,‖HN‖≤∑jN!(N−mj)!‖fj‖BW2≤dNmmaxj‖fj‖BW2.

Input model and resources. Generators with poly(n) monomials and poly(n)-bit coefficients; N given in unary (the tasks below are polynomial in N, never in logN). The register holds k=(k0,…,kn), ∑kj=N, in O(nlogN) qubits. HN has row sparsity ≤∑j(#monomials of fj)2 and entries ∑jfj,α‾fj,βk!(k−α+β)!/(k−α)!, computable reversibly in poly(n,logN) time, so a sparse-access block encoding with normalisation α=O(smax|Hxy|)=poly(n)Nmmax‖fj‖2 is available. QSVT then gives, at cost

T=poly(nm,d)×αΔN×log1ϵ

block-encoding uses, any of: P0 (projector onto (IN)⊥≅(R/I)N), Θ(HN) (projector onto IN), the polar isometries Uf, and eigenvalue-resolved access to HN. A degree N exponential in n is cheap in qubits only: α/ΔN and all precision targets scale polynomially in N, so the running time is exponential there. All promises below are on the normalised gap ΔN/α; the absolute ΔN changes under rescaling generators.

Task list, with the three cost factors (spectral α/ΔN; overlap, i.e. the weight of the prepared state on the target subspace; readout, cheap only for global linear-algebraic quantities).

  1. Distance to the ideal. Given a sparse f∈RN, estimate ⟨f|P0|f⟩/‖f‖2=distBW(f,IN)2/‖f‖2 to additive ϵ: cost O˜(T/ϵ) with amplitude estimation. This decides "f∈IN or f is ϵ-far" but is fundamentally an estimation problem, and its hardness (Conjecture 8.1) is stated as such. Exact membership is EXPSPACE-complete (Mayr–Meyer) only for unbounded degree; at fixed N it is linear algebra of dimension (N+nn), and the advantage is exponential in n only in the promise regime.
  2. Normalised Hilbert function. HF(N)/(N+nn)=TrP0/dimRN to additive ϵ, by DQC1-style trace estimation (a maximally mixed state on the composition register plus controlled QSVT; membership in DQC1 proper is not claimed), cost O˜(T/ϵ2). Exact or multiplicative estimates are out of reach (Section 6). The normalised value is informative for low codimension: for codimV=c, degV=D and N in the stable range, HF(N)/(N+nn)≈Dn!(n−c)!N−c, so c and D are readable when c=O(1) and the error is below 12n!(n−c)!N−c after controlling lower-order terms.
  3. Random elements. Applying P0 to the maximally mixed state of RN yields the mixed state P0/HF(N) (not a Haar-random pure quotient element, which is not efficiently preparable); applying Θ(HN) yields PIN/dimIN. Sampling monomials from these is a "random Macaulay row" sampler whose classical analogue is trivial, so no advantage.
  4. Quantum Stickelberger (direction, not a claim). For I saturated with R/I a zero-dimensional scheme of length D and z0 a non-zero-divisor, HF(N)=D for N≥reg and the compressed operators Zj=P0,N+1aj†P0,N satisfy Zj=Z0Xj with Xj the multiplication by xj=zj/z0 transported to (IN)⊥; the eigenvalues of Xj are the coordinates of the points (Stickelberger), with Jordan blocks at non-reduced points. The Xj commute but are not normal, so phase estimation does not apply; a QSVT singular-value scan of Zj−λZ0 is the natural substitute, with cost controlled by pseudospectral/eigenvector conditioning, not by coordinate separation alone. The setting is attractive because D may be exponential in n while the register is O(nlogN) qubits; the hardness sits in the overlap factor (Conjecture 8.6).
  5. Adiabatic preparation via Gröbner degeneration. Take a weight w and generators g1,…,gr forming a w-Gröbner basis of I with inw(gj) single monomials (generic w). Scale each monomial zα of gj by tmaxα′w⋅α′−w⋅α, t∈[0,1]. By Eisenbud Thm 15.17 (after t↦1/t) this is a flat family with I0=inw(I) precisely because the gj are a Gröbner basis; for an arbitrary generating set the t=0 fibre (inwfj) can be smaller than inw(I) and the kernel dimension jumps up there. For t>0 the family is a diagonal coordinate change, so constancy of dimIN(t) is trivial; the content is t=0, where HN(0) is diagonal with ground states the standard monomials and ΔN(0)≥minj|cj|2α(j)! in terms of the leading coefficients cj (presentation-dependent: rescaling a generator changes the gap, not the ideal). Numerically (twisted cubic, whose 2×2 minors are a Gröbner basis for w=(0,1,4,9), N≤9): kernel dimension 3N+1 for all t∈[10−10,1], ΔN(t) decreasing monotonically from ≈0.85N at t=1 to exactly 1 at t=0, so mintΔN(t)=1 independent of N and mintΔN(t)/‖HN(t)‖≈2.5N−1.75. Adiabatic evolution transports one initial standard monomial at a time into a ground state; the transported basis is related to any canonical basis of (R/I)N by the non-abelian holonomy of the path, and the degenerate-ground-space adiabatic theorem applies because the kernel dimension is constant. This "adiabatic Gröbner deformation" looks genuinely new.
  6. Homotopy paths. For ft=(1−t)g+tf the Hilbert function of R/It is upper semicontinuous in t: it can be larger at isolated special t (an excited eigenvalue of HN(t) touches zero there) and drops back immediately after. Complex t is allowed (HN(t) stays Hermitian positive). But constancy of the Hilbert function does not see root collisions (the scheme length is constant while roots merge), so this is not the discriminant avoidance of Beltrán–Pardo/Lairez; what an adiabatic root tracker would need is control of the spectrum of the compressed multiplication operators along the path, which is a different and harder question.

5.7 What the distance-to-ideal quantity is, geometrically. The quantity in task 1, δ(f)2:=⟨f|P0|f⟩/‖f‖2, looks artificial only if one insists on exact membership. It has several standard readings.

  • Quotient norm. δ(f)=‖[f]‖ for the Hilbert norm on (R/I)N induced from Bombieri–Weyl, i.e. the norm of the Drury–Arveson quotient module Hn+12/[I].
  • Restriction norm of a section to V. The map f↦(p↦⟨p⊗N|f⟩) is an isometry onto the reproducing-kernel space of the kernel ⟨p,q⟩N, and for radical I in the stable range P0 is the projector onto the closed span of the kernel functions at points of V (Aronszajn). Hence
δ(f)2=‖f|V‖RKHS(V)2‖f‖2≍NdimV−n∫V|f|hN2dvolV∫Pn|f|hN2dvol,

with comparison constants from Bergman-kernel asymptotics (Tian–Zelditch–Catlin) and Ohsawa–Takegoshi extension. The promise "f∈I or δ(f)≥ϵ" says the hypersurface {f=0} either contains V or misses it by a definite L2 amount, a Łojasiewicz-type or quantitative-Nullstellensatz separation. P0 itself is the degree-N Bergman projector of V written in ambient monomial coordinates, so Section 5 is quantum access to the Bergman kernel of a projective variety.

  • Polynomial-kernel machine learning. The Bombieri–Weyl space is the reproducing-kernel space of the homogeneous polynomial kernel (x⋅y)N. For a finite point set X, δ(f)2‖f‖2=fX†G−1fX with G the coherent-state Gram matrix and fX the evaluation vector; its diagonal approximation ∑x∈X|f(x)|2 is the objective of approximate vanishing ideals and Vanishing Component Analysis (Heldt–Kreuzer–Pokutta–Poulisse 2009; Livni et al. 2013), where "distance to the vanishing ideal of the data" is the working notion.
  • Ground-space overlap. In Hamiltonian-complexity terms δ(f)2 is the weight of a guiding state on the ground space of a gapped frustration-free Hamiltonian, the guided-local-Hamiltonian family of problems (Gharibian–Le Gall 2022 and successors), which is the source of the BQP intuition in Conjecture 8.1.

Once P0 is the Bergman projector, its bilinear and trace versions are geometric quantities that classical methods reach only through root-finding:

  • coherent inputs give the Berezin symbol ⟨p⊗N|P0|p⊗N⟩, a smooth indicator of V at Fubini–Study resolution N−1/2 whose Tian–Zelditch expansion NdimVπ−dimV(1+S(p)/2N+…) carries the scalar curvature S of V in the induced Fubini–Study metric (classically easy by differentiating the equations, so no advantage);
  • two ideals give Tr(PIPJ), which localises on V∩W at scale N−1/2: its growth exponent in N is dim(V∩W), and for disjoint varieties it decays like cos2N of the Fubini–Study distance between them, so the normalised overlap estimates intersection dimension or the distance between two varieties without forming I+J;
  • Toeplitz traces Tr(P0Tg)/HF(N) with g(z,z¯) a polynomial, so that Tg is a normal-ordered bosonic observable, converge by Berezin–Toeplitz theory (Bordemann–Meinrenken–Schlichenmaier) to ∫VgdvolV/vol(V): quantum integration over an algebraic variety.

The fair classical competitor for all three is Monte Carlo on V: cut with a random linear space of complementary dimension, whose intersection points are Fubini–Study-distributed on V by the kinematic formula, find them by homotopy continuation, and average. Its cost is governed by the condition number of the slice systems (average polynomial for Kostlan-random data, Beltrán–Pardo and Lairez), the quantum cost by α/ΔN. Whether these two condition numbers are related is the research question this analysis points to (Conjecture 8.11).

6. Where speedups cannot come from (hardness anchors)

  • Exact Hilbert function is #P-hard. The boolean ideal J=(zi2−ziz0)i=1n is a radical, saturated complete intersection of n quadrics with HF(N)=∑i≤min(N,n)(ni), equal to 2n from N=n on (numerically confirmed for n≤5), and (R/J)N is the full function algebra on {0,1}n for N≥n. Adjoining homogenised cubic clause forms of a 3-CNF ϕ (a clause form vanishing at [1:x] iff the clause is satisfied) gives an ideal K whose dehomogenisation is radical with V={[1:x]:x⊨ϕ}; the homogeneous K is generally neither radical nor saturated (irrelevant torsion), but since cubic multiples generate all functions supported on unsatisfying assignments once N≥n+3, HFR/K(N)=#SAT(ϕ) for N≥n+3 (numerically: 45 random and constructed instances with n=3,…,7, exact Gröbner/GF(p) ranks; stabilisation happens by N=n+2, and for N below that the Hilbert function overshoots, e.g. 29,57,71,57,29,9,2,1 for a unique-solution instance with n=7). So an exact quantum evaluation of HF would give #P⊆FBQP, and a multiplicative approximation NP⊆BQP. Already for squarefree monomial ideals the Hilbert series is #P-hard to evaluate (standard monomials of a graph ideal are independent sets; Dickenstein–Tobis 2012), and there HN is diagonal. The DQC1-style additive version is the only realistic target.
  • Kernel non-triviality. kerHN≠0⟺ϕ satisfiable is NP-hard; kerHN=0 is coNP-hard; via Proposition 3.1 the promise version is QMA1-hard. No spectral-gap promise rescues decision of frustration-freeness. Hilbert's Nullstellensatz problem (non-emptiness of V over C) is NP-hard and in AM under GRH (Koiran 1996), emptiness in coAM; it becomes "kerHN=0" at N∼dn (Kollár), representable but exponentially expensive in time.
  • Overlap factors. For a task solved by black-box projection of a prepared state onto a subspace of dimension HF(N), the cost is at least the inverse root of the state's weight on that subspace; from the maximally mixed state this is dimRN/HF(N). This is not a universal lower bound: structured ideals may admit efficiently preparable states with large weight on the ground space (Conjecture 8.6 uses exactly this).
  • Prior art on HHL for Macaulay matrices. Chen–Gao (2018/2022) run an HHL solver on a Macaulay linear system over C encoding a Boolean system, with cost polynomial in the condition number; Ding–Gheorghiu–Gilyén–Hallgren–Li (Quantum 2023) prove that this condition number is exponentially large unless the solution has Hamming weight O(logn), so Grover wins. Their system is a different Macaulay matrix (a solve for a particular vector) and there is no established reduction between their condition number and the quantities here; but the lesson is the same: in the hard instances the cost hides in a normalisation, not in a spectral gap, and any claim here must survive that test. The related "bosonic quantum computational complexity" framework (Chabaud–Joseph–Mehraban–Motamedi 2024) concerns unbounded-particle bosonic models and is different from the fixed-N symmetric sector here.
  • Fixed mode number is classically easy. Permutation-invariant Hamiltonians on N qudits of fixed local dimension have ground states and expectation values computable classically in time polynomial in N by Schur-basis block diagonalisation (Anschuetz–Bauer–Kiani–Lloyd 2023). Here the local dimension is n+1, so this covers exactly the regime "fixed ideal, growing N" and confirms independently that any advantage must come from growing n.
  • Dequantisation. HN is sparse and high-rank, so low-rank dequantisation does not apply to it; P0 has rank HF(N), polynomial in N for bounded dimV but possibly exponential in n through the degree, and classical low-rank methods need sample-and-query access and conditioning besides rank.

7. The central unknown: the Macaulay gap ΔN

Everything with a potential exponential speedup is governed by

ΔN=min{‖∑jfjhj‖2∑j‖hj‖2:(hj)∈⨁jRN−mj,(hj)⊥Syz(I)N},

a quantitative syzygy constant: how close a non-syzygy can come to being one. For one generator this is the Bombieri–Beauzamy–Enflo–Montgomery inequality [fh]BW≥m!(N−m)!/N![f]BW[h]BW, which in Fock normalisation reads ‖fh‖≥‖f‖‖h‖:

Fact 7.1 (principal ideals are uniformly gapped). For I=(f), ΔN≥‖f‖Fock2=m!‖f‖BW2 for all N≥m. The bound is attained whenever, after a unitary change of variables, the extra degree can be placed in a linear subspace orthogonal to the variables f depends on (e.g. f=z0m, h=z1N−m; or f=z0+z1, h=(z0−z1)N−1); in one variable ΔN=N!/(N−m)!. For the conic, a(f)a†(f)=a†(f)a(f)+nˆ0+nˆ1+4nˆ2+3 gives ΔN≥N+1, and numerically ΔN=N+1 exactly for N≤30.

Fact 7.2 (quadrics: the gap grows at a rate set by the distance to the cones). Any quadric can be brought by a unitary change of variables (Takagi factorisation of its complex symmetric matrix) to f=∑jdjwj2 with dj≥0 the Takagi values and wj orthonormal linear forms; this preserves the Bombieri–Weyl norm and turns bj=a(wj) into canonical modes. Then [a(f),a†(f)]=∑jdj2(4nˆj+2), and since a(f)a†(f)=a†(f)a(f)+[a(f),a†(f)] on RN−2,

4dmin2(N−2)+‖f‖Fock2≤ΔN≤dmin2N(N−1)+‖f‖Fock2−2dmin2,

the upper bound from h=wminN−2. For the conic, d=(1/2,1/2,1) and the lower bound is N+1, which is the exact value found numerically. So the gap of a quadric grows at least linearly with slope 4dmin2, where dmin is the Bombieri–Weyl distance from f to the quadrics of deficient rank, i.e. to the cones; a cone (dmin=0) has bounded gap. This explains the "flat" random quadric in P4 in the survey (a 5×5 Gaussian symmetric matrix typically has a small Takagi value, so the growth is slow) and the pinned pyramid example below.

For several generators no such inequality seems to be in the literature. ΔN depends on the generating tuple, not only on I: (f1,f1+ϵf2) generates the same ideal as (f1,f2) for ϵ≠0 and has ΔN=O(ϵ2). What is known or easy:

  • monomial ideals: HN is diagonal with integer entries ∑j∏iki!/(ki−αi(j))!, so ΔN≥1; e.g. ‖HN‖=N(N−1)(N−2) exactly for (z02,z0z1,z13);
  • binomial (toric) ideals: HN is exactly block diagonal over the fibres of the A-grading; each block is a weighted graph Laplacian on the fibre whose one-dimensional kernel is spanned by the fibre sum qu=∑Ak=uzk/k! (the exponential generating function of the fibre, numerically exact), so the degeneracy is the number of fibres and ΔN=minuλ2(Hu). The fibre graphs get denser faster than they get longer: for the twisted cubic ΔN/N→0.854 and for the rational normal quartic ΔN≈0.61N1.05, not the naive N−2. But growth is not general: for the toric ideal (x00x11−x01x10) of the pyramid over a square, with apex variable y absent from the relation, h=yN−2 attains Fact 7.1 and ΔN=2 for all N (numerically exact for N≤8; the same binomial without y has ΔN=N exactly, and adjoining an unused variable to the twisted cubic flattens its gap from 0.85N to exactly 2 for N≤12). Any lower bound growing with N must therefore exclude ideals extended from a subring;
  • Bombieri–Weyl norms are U(n+1)-invariant, so ΔN is a unitary invariant of the generator tuple, the quantity that would appear in a Shub–Smale/Bürgisser–Cucker condition theory for the Macaulay map.

At fixed N and growing n: the boolean ideal has Δn+1=2 for n=2,…,6 (numerically, not a theorem), and random quadric ideals at N=3,4 have Δ≈0.7–1.2, flat in n. For every "algebraic-geometry" family tested, ΔN is bounded below by a constant and ‖HN‖/ΔN grows like N1 to N3.

The gap does close in the multigraded QSAT sector: a chain of nearest-neighbour singlet projectors on m qubits has Δ=9.13m−1.97 (m2Δ→π2, the ferromagnetic Heisenberg gap), and random frustration-free 2-local chains have median Δ≈m−3.5 over m≤11 (a power law fits better than an exponential, but m≤11 cannot exclude a slow exponential). Since the multigraded sector realises every frustration-free projector Hamiltonian, Δ inherits every known pathology, including exponentially small gaps (Proposition 8.3(d)). The numerics suggest a dichotomy: fixed ideal and growing N is benign; growing n with spin-chain-like generators is where hardness lives.

8. What is a theorem, what is a conjecture

Theorems (some new as statements, none deep). Facts 2.1–2.3, 4.1, 4.2, 7.1, 7.2; Propositions 3.1 and 8.2; the identifications of 5.7 (quotient norm, RKHS restriction norm, polynomial-kernel norm); the coherent-state characterisation span{|p⟩⊗N:p¯∈V}=(I(V)N)⊥ and hence "kerHN is spanned by product states iff IN=I(V)N"; the toric fibre-sum description of the ground space; flatness and constant kernel dimension along a Gröbner-basis degeneration; Arveson's curvature = leading multiplicity of the Hilbert polynomial (Arveson 2000, Fang 2003).

Conjecture 8.1 (distance-to-ideal estimation is BQP-hard and in BQP). Input: multi-homogeneous generators of degree ≤m=O(1) in poly(n) variables with poly(n)-bit coefficients of unit Bombieri–Weyl norm, a multidegree sector (or total degree N≤poly(n)), a poly(n)-sparse unit vector f in that sector, thresholds a<b with b−a≥1/poly(n), and the promise Δ/α≥1/poly(n) for the Hamiltonian restricted to that sector. Decide whether ⟨f|P0|f⟩≤a or ≥b. Containment is Section 5.1 (the block encoding can be restricted to the invariant sector). Hardness sketch: embed the Feynman–Kitaev Hamiltonian Hin+Hprop of a BQP circuit U=UT⋯U1 into the (1,…,1) sector via Section 3; it is frustration-free with unique ground state (the history state) and gap Ω(T−2) in that sector; for the normalised two-monomial states f±=(|0,clock=0⟩±|y,clock=T⟩)/2, ⟨f±|P0|f±⟩=|1±⟨y|U|0⟩‾|2/(2(T+1)), whose difference is 2Re⟨y|U|0⟩/(T+1), and deciding the sign of Re⟨y|U|0⟩ against a 1/poly threshold is BQP-hard (the standard matrix-element problem). Degree m=5 should suffice; m=2 is plausible via 2-local frustration-free universality gadgets. This is hardness for estimating the distance; the exact "member or ϵ-far" problem is not what the reduction produces.

Proposition 8.2 (bosonic QSAT is QMA1-hard). The symmetric-sector analogue of QSAT (ordinary grading, N=n bosons in 2n modes, generators of degree ≤3, promise version) is QMA1-hard. Reduction: take a Gosset–Nagaj instance on n qubits as multilinear cubic generators in the site-block variables zi,s, and add the hard-core generators zi,szi,s′ for all i and all s≤s′ (squares included). Every generator preserves the per-site occupation pattern, so Hn is block diagonal in that pattern; on the pattern (1,…,1) the hard-core terms vanish and the block is exactly the QSAT Hamiltonian (Section 3); on every other pattern some site holds ≥2 bosons and the diagonal hard-core part alone is ≥1. Hence kerHn is the QSAT ground space and the nonzero spectrum is ≥min(ΔQSAT,1). (Without the hard-core terms the construction fails: any state with all bosons in one block is a spurious zero mode.)

Conjecture 8.3 (gap classes). Fock normalisation, unit Bombieri–Weyl generators, N≥maxmj:

(a) Uniform Bombieri inequality for ideals. For every fixed generating tuple, infNΔN>0: there is c(f1,…,fd)>0 with ‖∑jfjhj‖2≥cdist((hj),SyzN)2 for all N. This is Fact 7.1 for d=1, holds for monomial ideals with monic generators with c=minjα(j)! (a lower bound, not necessarily infNΔN), and is consistent with all numerics. It is a closed-range statement for the Macaulay map on the graded Fock module and is genuinely open. Consequence: α/ΔN=poly(N) for every fixed ideal, so the Section 5 tasks are polynomial in N at fixed I (with N in unary), and quantum advantage is entirely a question of the dependence on n and the generator structure.

(b) Cones versus growth. If I is extended from a subring, i.e. after a unitary change of variables the generators omit a variable w (so V is a cone with vertex the point dual to w), then hj=wN−mj shows ΔN≤minj‖fj‖2 up to the syzygy correction: the gap is pinned to a constant (the pyramid example, and the twisted cubic with a dummy variable, both numerically flat at 2). Conjecture: conversely, for a hypersurface ΔN→∞ linearly iff V(f) is not a cone, i.e. iff the partials ∂0f,…,∂nf are linearly independent, with slope controlled by the Bombieri–Weyl distance from f to the locus of cones (for quadrics this is Fact 7.2 with slope 4dmin2); and for toric ideals ΔN=minuλ2(Hu), a weighted Poincaré constant of the fibre graphs, grows linearly iff the toric variety is not a cone.

(c) Generic forms. For d≤n+1 independent Kostlan-random forms of degree m normalised to unit Bombieri–Weyl norm, Pr[infN≥mΔN≥c(m)]→1 as n→∞, with c(m)>0 independent of n. Evidence is thin (flat in n at N=3,4; ΔN≥0.1N0.66 for two generic quadrics in P3). The natural proof route is a least-singular-value estimate for the structured random Macaulay map in the Bombieri–Weyl basis, where U(n+1)-invariance makes the distribution tractable.

(d) Hard instances exist (essentially a theorem). For every fixed m≥2 there are ideals generated by poly(n) forms of degree m with Δ/α≤2−Ω(n) in a multigraded sector specified by polynomially many sites (of dimension qn): the area-weighted Motzkin chains of Levine–Movassagh (2017) are 2-local, frustration-free, with gap ≤8nst−n2/3, and the multigraded embedding transfers the spectrum; for m>2 pad each term by a complete rank-one decomposition of the identity on m−2 auxiliary sites. So there is no general polynomial-time quantum algorithm for the Section 5 tasks without a gap promise. Random frustration-free 2-local chains appear to be only polynomially gapped (≈m−3.5 numerically), so typical multigraded instances are not the hard ones.

(e) Presentation dependence (question, not conjecture). c in (a) is a property of the tuple. Is there a lower bound on infNΔN in terms of finitely many degrees, e.g. minN≤N0ΔN with N0=reg(I)+maxmj, or in terms of the Bombieri–Weyl distance of the tuple to the locus of tuples with an extra syzygy in degrees ≤N0? Whether infNΔN is attained at finite N or only asymptotically is open in both directions.

Conjecture 8.4 (adiabatic Gröbner deformation). Fix a reduced w-Gröbner basis G of I with unit Bombieri–Weyl elements and the one-parameter degeneration of 5.5. (a) For toric ideals, determinantal ideals of generic matrices, and complete intersections of d≤n forms, mint∈[0,1]ΔN(t) is attained at the monomial end t=0, where ΔN(0)≥minj|cj|2α(j)! (cj the leading coefficients; equality is not general, e.g. f=x2y+xy2 with wx>wy has Δ4(0)=4|c|2), so mintΔN(t) is bounded below independently of N while ‖HN(t)‖=O(Nm); hence each standard monomial can be transported adiabatically to a ground state of HN in time poly(N). Evidence: the twisted cubic, mintΔN(t)=ΔN(0)=1 for N=3,…,9, with ΔN(t) monotone in t. Neither flatness nor a fixed ideal gives spectral monotonicity in t by itself. (b) Define the adiabatic Gröbner complexity of I at degree N as minwmaxt∈[0,1]α(t)/ΔN(t), the minimum over weight vectors w in the open cones of the Gröbner fan (monomial initial ideal), with G the corresponding reduced Gröbner basis normalised to unit Bombieri–Weyl norm and α(t) the block-encoding normalisation. Conjecture: it is polynomial in (n,N) for the classes in (a) and exponential in n for the instances of 8.3(d).

Conjecture 8.5 (entangled defect). The theorem part is in the list above; the conjectural part is descriptive: for zero-dimensional I in the stable range (where irrelevant torsion has vanished), dimI(V)N−dimIN=length(ProjR/I)−#V, and the ground-space vectors orthogonal to all coherent states of points of V are spanned by the local inverse systems at the non-reduced points, i.e. by the jets D(∂p)|p⟩⊗N of coherent states along the differential functionals in Macaulay's local dual at each point (first derivatives for curvilinear double points, higher jets in general). A quantum radicality test does not follow: the coherent states of the points are non-orthogonal with a Gram matrix that can be exponentially ill-conditioned, and a root-sampling oracle does not give the projector onto their span.

Conjecture 8.6 (root sampling is Grover-limited). For the boolean-plus-clause ideal K of Section 6 with D solutions and N≥n+3: (i) ΔN(K)/α≥1/poly(n). Numerically, at N=n+3 over 54 instances with n=3,…,8 (random 3-CNF at clause ratios 2 and 4.2, UNSAT instances, unique-solution instances) the smallest nonzero eigenvalue stays in [0.29,2.3] while 2n grows by 32×; exponential and power-law fits are both statistically empty (R2≤0.16), and the pure boolean part has Δ=2 throughout. The normalised quantity ‖HN‖/ΔN does grow, 2.8×103→2.0×104, fitting n2.1 and e0.43n about equally well over six points, because ‖HN‖≈4.2n cubics of norm N3; so the gap is not where the hardness is, but the normalised-gap conjecture is still only supported, not established; (ii) for N=Ω(nlog(n/ε)) the Gram matrix of the 2n coherent states |pˆx⟩⊗N, pˆx=(1,x)/1+|x|, is ε-close to the identity in operator norm (nearest neighbours have overlap ≈e−N/(2n), and the number of neighbours at Hamming distance r is (nr)), so Ψ∝∑x|pˆx⟩⊗N is normalised by 2−n/2 up to O(ε) (the logarithmic factor is necessary: numerically the smallest Gram eigenvalue of the full hypercube family is 3.5e−0.85n at N=n+3 and still 0.7e−0.35n at N=2n, while the Gram matrix restricted to the satisfying assignments of a random instance is well conditioned, λmin≥0.13; at N=n+3 the weight ⟨Ψ|P0|Ψ⟩ exceeds D/2n by factors 1–12); Ψ is preparable to trace distance O(ε) by preparing ∑x|x⟩|pˆx⟩⊗N and uncomputing x from the occupation pattern, which determines it except with probability ≤ne−N/(n+1); and Ψ has weight D/2n+O(ε) on kerHN(K); (iii) hence projecting Ψ onto the ground space and reading a root off the occupation pattern samples a solution, uniformly up to O(ε), in time poly(n,N)⋅α/ΔN⋅2n/D. This is an upper bound achieving Grover's scaling for this projection method; no lower bound against other algorithms is claimed. Starting instead from the maximally mixed state gives weight D/(2n+3n)≈D4−n, which is worse than Grover; the earlier draft got this wrong. The Chen–Gao/Ding et al. obstruction is the analogous statement for their Macaulay linear system.

Conjecture 8.7 (normalised Hilbert function and low codimension). For ideals with codimV=c=O(1), regularity ≤poly(n) and N≥poly(n) in the stable range, a DQC1-style estimate of HF(N)/dimRN to additive error below 12n!(n−c)!N−c at a few values of N determines c and degV in time poly(n,N,α/ΔN), polynomial for the classes in 8.3(c). Classically, the Hilbert polynomial of d≤n+1 generic forms is known (Koszul), so any advantage is for structured low-codimension ideals with many generators and non-trivial syzygies, whose Hilbert functions are not known in closed form (the regime of Fröberg's conjecture, though the additive precision is far too coarse to test that conjecture).

Conjecture 8.8 (finite fields: no compact analogue). Macaulay's duality survives over Fq via the divided-power/contraction action (ordinary differentiation fails, ∂xp=0), and it is realised quantumly as Fourier duality between the uniform superpositions over IN and over its contraction-dual, on the additive group FqdimRN (with the syzygy module as the hidden subgroup of the explicitly known map (hj)↦∑hjfj, which yields no advantage over finite-field Gaussian elimination). That register has dimRNlogq qubits. The compact amplitude encoding of the complex construction cannot be transported because Fq does not embed in C as a field, so complex linear algebra does not preserve Fq-ranks or products; the realistic finite-field analogue is HHL/QSVT on complex Macaulay systems that encode Fq-constraints (Chen–Gao), with the Ding et al. obstruction. This is the one direction in the notes that I think does not work.

Conjecture 8.9 (operator-theoretic dictionary as a conditioning statement). The compressed shifts Zj on the ground spaces commute exactly; the Arveson–Douglas conjecture says [Zi,Zj†] is Schatten-p for p>dimV+1 (affine-cone dimension), i.e. the tuple is asymptotically normal as N→∞. Conjecture (zero-dimensional case only, where Xj=μz0−1μzj is defined on a fixed stabilised ground space): for a reduced zero-dimensional scheme with z0 a non-zero-divisor, the eigenvector condition number of the commuting tuple (Xj) on (IN)⊥, N≥reg, is bounded by a polynomial in N, the inverse coordinate separation of the points, the coefficient height and the presentation conditioning α/ΔN; for non-reduced points it degrades with the local multiplicity through the Jordan structure. This is what would make the direction 5.4 well-conditioned in the reduced case. Essential normality alone gives no finite-N bound, and ordinary phase estimation does not apply to non-normal Zj, so this is a conjecture about pseudospectra, not a consequence of the Arveson–Douglas theory.

Conjecture 8.10 (geometric overlaps). For radical I,J with smooth V,W in the stable range: (a) Tr(PIPJ)=cNdim(V∩W)(1+O(1/N)) when V∩W≠∅ is transversal, with c a positive combination of the degrees of the components of V∩W and angle factors; (b) Tr(PIPJ)≤HFI(N)HFJ(N)cos2NdFS(V,W) when V∩W=∅, with −12NlogTr(PIPJ)→logsecdFS(V,W); (c) for g a polynomial in z,z¯, Tr(P0Tg)/HF(N)=1volV∫VgdvolV+O(1/N) with the 1/N term given by the Berezin–Toeplitz expansion (Laplacian and scalar-curvature corrections). All three are DQC1-style estimable in time poly(n,N,α/ΔN,1/ϵ) to additive ϵ in the normalised form; (a) and (c) are informative for low codimension.

Conjecture 8.11 (Macaulay gap versus condition number). For a reduced complete intersection V of d≤n forms of degree m with unit Bombieri–Weyl norm and Bürgisser–Cucker condition number μmax(V)=supp∈Vμnorm(f,p), the normalised gap satisfies ΔN/α≥1/poly(n,N,μmax); conversely, singular V (infinite μmax) forces ΔN/α→0 faster than any power of N only through cone-type degenerations, so that lim infNΔN>0 still holds (Conjecture 8.3(a)). If true, the quantum cost of the Bergman-projector tasks of 5.7 and the classical cost of Monte Carlo on V by random slicing and homotopy continuation are controlled by the same condition quantity, and the comparison reduces to polynomial factors.
